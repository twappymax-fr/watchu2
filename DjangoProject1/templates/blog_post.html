When the Floods Came:
Adapting Livelihoods to a Changing Climate

Miriam Odhiambo Field Correspondent, Torvu Tanzania June 2026 8 min read

In the low-lying delta villages of Rufiji, the rains no longer arrive on schedule. For Amara Mwalimu, a smallholder farmer who has tended the same plot of land for twenty years, the change has been impossible to ignore. Three floods in four years have inundated her maize fields, washed away months of labour, and pushed her family to the edge of a crisis that no single harvest could fix.

Climate change hits hardest those who contributed least to it. In sub-Saharan Africa, where agriculture employs the majority of the rural poor and rainfall patterns are shifting with little warning, the consequences are not abstract — they are counted in lost harvests, withdrawn children from school, and empty plates.

Amara Mwalimu on her land in Rufiji delta, now partially converted to raised-bed vegetable plots that survive seasonal flooding. Photo: Torvu / Miriam Odhiambo, 2026.

The Season Everything Changed

It was March 2023 when the water came for the third time. Amara had already planted — maize, beans, a small patch of cassava — and the seedlings were just beginning to show. Within two days, the fields were under half a metre of brown water. She lost everything she had planted.

What followed was a period of extraordinary difficulty. Her husband took on casual labour in Dar es Salaam. Her eldest daughter was pulled from secondary school for a term. The family borrowed from neighbours to eat. "I felt like I was farming the same land my mother farmed," Amara told us, "but the land had become something else. Something I didn't recognise."

The land used to speak to us. We knew what it needed. Now it does things we have never seen before, and we are learning its language all over again.

Amara Mwalimu, smallholder farmer, Rufiji, Tanzania

Torvu's Climate-Resilient Agriculture Programme

In late 2023, Torvu's Tanzania field office began piloting a climate-resilient agriculture programme across twelve villages in the Rufiji delta. The intervention was designed not as a one-off handout but as a multi-season transformation of farming practice — combining raised-bed construction, flood-tolerant seed varieties, group savings schemes, and real-time weather alerts delivered via community radio and SMS.

Amara was among the first forty households enrolled. The process began not with seeds or tools, but with a community mapping exercise — a three-day workshop where farmers, extension workers, and local elders charted which plots flooded first, which slopes drained fastest, and where water had historically pooled. "We already knew all this," said one village elder. "But nobody had ever asked us to write it down."

Yield increase in the first full harvest cycle Programme participants using raised-bed flood-tolerant systems saw median yields triple compared to the same households' pre-programme baselines.

The technical training covered raised-bed construction — mounding earth above flood level, reinforced with bamboo stakes and vetiver grass to prevent erosion — and the introduction of flood-tolerant varieties of rice and leafy vegetables sourced through Torvu's agricultural supply chain. Crucially, the programme also worked with the local SACCOS (savings and credit cooperatives) to provide short-term seasonal loans, so that farmers who suffered partial losses could replant quickly without falling into debt spirals with informal lenders.

Left: Raised-bed construction in progress. Right: Community seed exchange — a cornerstone of the programme's knowledge-sharing model.

More Than a Farming Programme

What quickly became clear to the field team was that flood resilience is inseparable from financial resilience. A family that can survive one bad season without crisis — because they have savings, because they have diversified income, because their children stay in school — is a family that can invest in next season. Torvu extended the programme to include a dedicated women's enterprise track, supporting participants to start or grow small businesses — from charcoal production to dried fish trading — that would generate income during the months when farming is disrupted.

Amara now runs a small vegetable stall at the district market. Her raised beds produce amaranth, tomatoes, and onions through the wet season when her neighbours' fields are inundated. She has trained three other women in her village to build their own beds. Her daughter is back in school.

My grandmother farmed this same land. I want my daughter to farm it too. But she has to farm it differently than I did — smarter, more careful. The programme taught us that this is not giving up. It is adapting.

Amara Mwalimu

Women's enterprise training session, Rufiji district. Forty-three women completed the 2024–25 cohort, of whom 38 reported sustained secondary income six months after graduation.

What the Numbers Show

Twelve months into the programme, Torvu's monitoring team surveyed all forty households in the pilot cohort. The results were striking. Median annual household income had increased by 41%, driven primarily by vegetable sales and the women's enterprise track. Child school dropout rates in programme households fell from 22% to 3% over the pilot period. Every household reported completing their seasonal planting on time, compared to just over half the previous year.

The programme is now being expanded to sixty additional villages across Rufiji and the neighbouring Kilwa district, with support from Torvu's climate resilience fund and co-financing from the European Union. A formal randomised evaluation will begin in 2027 to assess long-term impact.

Back on her plot, Amara is harvesting the second crop from beds she built herself, with her own hands, from soil she knows. The floods have not stopped. But she has stopped being stopped by them.
